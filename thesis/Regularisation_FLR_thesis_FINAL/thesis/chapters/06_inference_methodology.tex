\chapter{Inference Methodology}
\label{chap:inference-methodology}

Chapter~\ref{chap:estimation-results} showed that the estimation performance of
CG--FPLS can rest on aggressive stopping.  For inference, the residual left by
that stopping rule becomes a potential first-order distortion.  The question
is therefore not whether regularisation remains useful, but how far the
Krylov path must be continued before its approximation error disappears
relative to sampling uncertainty.  Estimation balances approximation bias
against sampling variance in $\widehat\beta_m$.  The inferential procedure of
\textcite{babii_carrasco_tsafack_2026} instead requires the fitted empirical
moment $\widehat K\widehat\beta_m$ to reproduce $\widehat r$ to an error
smaller than the root-$n$ sampling fluctuation.  This chapter makes that
distinction operational.

The analysis concerns the moment-residual CG--FPLS path defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methods-cg}.  It is not a new comparison of all four
estimators from Chapter~\ref{chap:estimation-results}, and its conclusions do
not automatically extend to raw FPLS, Arnoldi response-space FPLS, or FPCR.
The analysis moves from the hypothesis and test statistic to the
late-stopping requirement, null law, power behaviour, and inverted confidence
set.  Its implementation is then fixed before the numerical evidence is read
in Chapter~\ref{chap:inference-results}.

\section{Inferential target and test statistic}
\label{sec:inference-target}

Retain the centred scalar-on-function model
\begin{equation}
    Y_i^c=\langle X_i^c,\beta\rangle+\varepsilon_i,
    \qquad
    \mathbb E(\varepsilon_i\mid X_i)=0,
    \label{eq:inference-model}
\end{equation}
and the normal equation $K\beta=r$.  The empirical moments
$\widehat K$ and $\widehat r$ are those in
\eqref{eq:methods-empirical-moments}; their grid implementation is given by
\eqref{eq:methods-grid-moments}.  For a known candidate function $b$, the
nominal hypothesis is
\begin{equation}
    H_0:\beta=b
    \qquad\text{against}\qquad
    H_1:\beta\neq b.
    \label{eq:inference-hypothesis}
\end{equation}
This is a global hypothesis about the slope function rather than a pointwise
claim about $\beta(s)$ at one argument.  Choosing $b=0$ gives the important
special case of no functional effect, but the procedure is not restricted to
that null.  In the Monte Carlo size experiment, $b$ is the known true slope of
the data-generating model.

The qualification from Section~\ref{sec:identification-illposedness} remains
essential.  When $K$ is not injective, the observations identify only
$K\beta$ and cannot distinguish functions that differ by an element of
$\operatorname{Null}(K)$.  The statistically meaningful unrestricted null is
therefore
\begin{equation}
    H_0:K\beta=Kb.
    \label{eq:inference-moment-null}
\end{equation}
If $\beta$ and $b$ are restricted to
$\mathcal H_{\mathrm{id}}=\operatorname{Null}(K)^\perp$, this moment null is
equivalent to \eqref{eq:inference-hypothesis}.  All references below to the
slope null are understood on this identified space.  In particular, no test
based on the functional linear model can have power against a perturbation
lying entirely in $\operatorname{Null}(K)$.

Let $\widehat\beta_m^{\mathrm{CG}}$ minimise the empirical moment residual over
the $m$th Krylov space as in \eqref{eq:methods-cg-moment-objective}.  It is
useful to make the iteration index explicit and write
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal T_{n,m}(b)
    =n\left\|
       \widehat K\bigl(\widehat\beta_m^{\mathrm{CG}}-b\bigr)
      \right\|^2.
    \label{eq:inference-finite-m-statistic}
\end{equation}
For an inference-specific sequence $m=m_n$, the shorter notation
$\mathcal T_n(b)=\mathcal T_{n,m_n}(b)$ recovers
\eqref{eq:intro-test-statistic}.  The symbol $T_n$ remains reserved for the
sampling operator introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:methods}; the calligraphic
symbol denotes the scalar test statistic.

Weighting the slope difference by $\widehat K$ is decisive.  Direct inference
on $\|\widehat\beta_m-b\|$ would inherit the unstable inversion of small
covariance eigenvalues.  By contrast,
$\widehat K(\widehat\beta_m-b)$ lives in moment space, where the sampling
fluctuation has a root-$n$ limit under ordinary moment conditions.  The
procedure consequently tests the covariance-visible part of the slope without
requiring an asymptotically regular estimator of the unweighted slope itself.

\section{Late stopping and the direct-moment statistic}
\label{sec:inference-late-stopping}

Define the null moment and the CG fitting residual by
\begin{equation}
    u_n(b)=\widehat r-\widehat Kb,
    \qquad
    e_m=\widehat r-\widehat K\widehat\beta_m^{\mathrm{CG}}.
    \label{eq:inference-two-residuals}
\end{equation}
The exact decomposition previewed in
\eqref{eq:inference-residual-decomposition-background} is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat K(\widehat\beta_m^{\mathrm{CG}}-b)
    =u_n(b)-e_m.
    \label{eq:inference-residual-decomposition}
\end{equation}
It separates statistical error in the empirical null moment from algorithmic
error left by terminating the iterative solution.  The corresponding
direct-moment statistic is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal S_n(b)
    =n\|u_n(b)\|^2
    =n\|\widehat r-\widehat Kb\|^2.
    \label{eq:inference-direct-statistic}
\end{equation}
Expanding the squared norm in \eqref{eq:inference-residual-decomposition}
gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal T_{n,m}(b)-\mathcal S_n(b)
    =-2n\langle u_n(b),e_m\rangle+n\|e_m\|^2,
    \label{eq:inference-statistic-difference}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 \left|\mathcal T_{n,m}(b)-\mathcal S_n(b)\right|
 \leq
 2\sqrt n\|u_n(b)\|\sqrt n\|e_m\|
 +n\|e_m\|^2.
 \label{eq:inference-statistic-bound}
\end{equation}

Under the null, $\sqrt n\|u_n(b)\|=O_{\mathbb P}(1)$.  It follows from
\eqref{eq:inference-statistic-bound} that the sufficient late-stopping
condition is
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt n\,
    \|\widehat r-\widehat K\widehat\beta_{m_n}^{\mathrm{CG}}\|
    =o_{\mathbb P}(1).
    \label{eq:inference-late-stopping-condition}
\end{equation}
When this condition holds,
$\mathcal T_{n,m_n}(b)=\mathcal S_n(b)+o_{\mathbb P}(1)$.  The detailed
argument appears in Proposition~\ref{prop:app-inference-decomposition} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:app-late-stopping}.

Condition \eqref{eq:inference-late-stopping-condition} is stricter than the
estimation rule in \eqref{eq:methods-cg-stopping-rule}.  The latter stops when
$\|e_m\|$ falls below a data-dependent constant multiple of $n^{-1/2}$; that
is appropriate for regularisation, but does not by itself make
$\sqrt n\|e_m\|$ vanish.  Continuing the path can therefore worsen the
unweighted slope estimate while improving the approximation relevant to the
test.  Inference asks for an accurate fitted moment, not an unregularised
slope estimate.

The theory does not prescribe a universal component count.  In finite samples
the required index depends on the covariance spectrum, realised Krylov path,
sample size, and numerical precision.  The paper's null-distribution
experiment and the present null-law and fixed-index stopping branches use
$m_{\mathrm{late}}=70$ as a conservative diagnostic choice at
$n=100$.  The power branch instead uses the direct-moment target, while the
confidence illustration uses the separate choice $m_C=10$.  The mathematical
requirement is
\eqref{eq:inference-late-stopping-condition}, not the number 70.  At the full
empirical Krylov dimension, the residual is zero in exact arithmetic whenever
$\widehat r$ lies in $\operatorname{Range}(\widehat K)$, but moving that far is
neither necessary for first-order inference nor attractive for slope
estimation.  The stopping experiment therefore examines the entire path
rather than treating a chosen index as self-validating.

\section{Null distribution and critical-value calibration}
\label{sec:inference-calibration}

Suppose the observations are independent and identically distributed,
$\mathbb E\|X^c\|^4<\infty$, and
$\mathbb E(\varepsilon^2\mid X)\leq\sigma_{\max}^2<\infty$.  Under
$H_0:K\beta=Kb$, and with the known zero means used in the simulations, the
empirical null moment is exactly
\begin{equation}
    u_n(b)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_iX_i^c.
    \label{eq:inference-null-score-average}
\end{equation}
If population means are replaced by sample means in an empirical application,
the corresponding centring term is asymptotically negligible under the stated
moment conditions.
Let the covariance operator of the random score $\varepsilon X^c$ be
\begin{equation}
    V
    =\mathbb E\!\left\{\varepsilon^2
       (X^c\otimes X^c)\right\},
    \qquad
    Vh=\mathbb E\!\left[\varepsilon^2
       \langle X^c,h\rangle X^c\right].
    \label{eq:inference-variance-operator}
\end{equation}
The Hilbert-space central limit theorem yields
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt n\,u_n(b)\rightsquigarrow G,
    \qquad G\sim\mathcal N_{\mathcal H}(0,V).
    \label{eq:inference-hilbert-clt}
\end{equation}
If $\{\omega_j\}$ are the positive eigenvalues of $V$, the spectral
representation of $G$ and Parseval's identity imply
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal T_n(b)
    \rightsquigarrow
    \|G\|^2
    =\sum_{j\geq1}\omega_jZ_j^2,
    \qquad Z_j\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,1).
    \label{eq:inference-weighted-chi-square}
\end{equation}
The series is well defined because $V$ is trace class under the stated moment
conditions.  Unlike an ordinary chi-square statistic with known degrees of
freedom, this distribution is not pivotal: its weights depend on the joint
distribution of $X$ and the regression error.

When the errors are conditionally homoskedastic,
$\mathbb E(\varepsilon^2\mid X)=\sigma^2$, then
\begin{equation}
    V=\sigma^2K,
    \qquad
    \omega_j=\sigma^2\lambda_j,
    \label{eq:inference-homoskedastic-weights}
\end{equation}
where $\{\lambda_j\}$ are the positive eigenvalues of $K$.  Under
heteroskedasticity, $V$ and $K$ need not share eigenfunctions.  The
homoskedastic simplification is proved in
Proposition~\ref{prop:app-homoskedastic-variance}.

Let $q_{1-\alpha}$ denote the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the law in
\eqref{eq:inference-weighted-chi-square}.  The level-$\alpha$ rule is
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_n(b)
    =\mathbf 1\{\mathcal T_n(b)>q_{1-\alpha}\}.
    \label{eq:inference-rejection-rule}
\end{equation}
Provided the limiting law is non-degenerate, it is continuous at its quantile,
and the rejection probability converges to $\alpha$ under the null.  The
formal statement is Corollary~\ref{cor:app-asymptotic-size}.

Three calibration concepts are relevant and play different roles.

\paragraph{Oracle weighted chi-square calibration.}
In this simulation the continuous-design score variances and error variance
are known.  The oracle benchmark draws independent $Z_j$ and simulates
\begin{equation}
    Q^{\mathrm{oracle}}
    =\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sigma^2\lambda_jZ_j^2,
    \label{eq:inference-oracle-law}
\end{equation}
then uses its empirical quantile.  The reported experiment uses $50{,}000$ such
draws.  The label \emph{oracle} means that unknown population information is
supplied by the data-generating design.  It does not mean that the resulting
test is exact at finite $n$.  The implemented weights describe the continuous
design; the endpoint-inclusive, equally weighted grid only approximates its
orthonormality.  To make the distinction precise, let
$\Phi=(\phi_j(s_t))_{j,t}\in\mathbb R^{J\times T}$,
$G_T=T^{-1}\Phi\Phi^\top$, and
$\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_J)$.
Under the grid inner product, the non-zero covariance eigenvalues are those
of $\Lambda^{1/2}G_T\Lambda^{1/2}$.  Writing these eigenvalues as
$\nu_{T,j}$, with zeros included if necessary, gives the fixed-grid limit
\begin{equation}
    Q_T=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sigma^2\nu_{T,j}Z_j^2.
    \label{eq:inference-grid-oracle-law}
\end{equation}
With $T=200$ held fixed, increasing $n$ does not remove the difference between
$\nu_{T,j}$ and $\lambda_j$.  For fixed $J$, grid refinement gives
$G_T\to I_J$ and recovers the continuous-design reference.  The stored
experiment uses \eqref{eq:inference-oracle-law}, so its calibration discrepancy
combines finite-sample effects, grid approximation, and Monte Carlo error in
the simulated reference distribution and quantile.  It does not isolate
finite-sample error relative to the exact fixed-grid limit.

Independent oracle draws are generated for each model.  Consequently, two
models with the same theoretical weight
sequence can receive slightly different simulated quantiles; such a difference
is calibration Monte Carlo error rather than a substantive change in the null
law.

\paragraph{Empirical-null calibration.}
When repeated null samples are available in a Monte Carlo experiment, one can
take the desired quantile of their realised null statistics.  This avoids
relying on the continuous-design weighted chi-square approximation, while
retaining Monte Carlo uncertainty in the estimated critical value.  It is
used for the reported power curves, separately for each
model and sample size.  Such calibration is also simulation-specific: a real
dataset does not provide thousands of independent samples generated under a
known null.

\paragraph{Feasible calibration for data analysis.}
With observed data, the nuisance distribution must be estimated.  For a
specified null, define the observable null residual
$\widehat\varepsilon_i(b)=Y_i^c-\langle X_i^c,b\rangle$.  One route replaces
$V$ by the covariance estimator
\begin{equation}
    \widehat V
    =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}
      \widehat\varepsilon_i(b)^2
      (X_i^c\otimes X_i^c)
    \label{eq:inference-plugin-variance}
\end{equation}
and simulates a weighted chi-square variable using its estimated eigenvalues.
Alternatively, a non-parametric bootstrap can approximate the null law.  These
two feasible directions are discussed by
\textcite{babii_carrasco_tsafack_2026}.  The supplied Python program also
contains an exploratory centred-multiplier option, but the stored archive uses
only the oracle benchmark for null size and confidence-set calculations.
Consequently, Chapter~\ref{chap:inference-results} makes no empirical claim
about the unreported feasible calibrations.

\section{Alternatives and power}
\label{sec:inference-power}

Under a fixed alternative with $K(\beta-b)\neq0$, consistency follows from
\begin{equation}
    \frac1n\mathcal T_n(b)
    \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}
    \|K(\beta-b)\|^2>0.
    \label{eq:inference-fixed-alternative}
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathcal T_n(b)$ diverges and the probability of rejection approaches
one.  The qualification $K(\beta-b)\neq0$ again excludes observationally
invisible null-space directions.

Non-trivial asymptotic power is described by the triangular sequence
\begin{equation}
    H_{1,n}:\quad
    \beta_n=b+n^{-1/2}\Delta,
    \qquad \Delta\in\mathcal H.
    \label{eq:inference-local-alternative}
\end{equation}
Under the same late-stopping condition,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal T_n(b)
    \rightsquigarrow
    \|G+K\Delta\|^2.
    \label{eq:inference-local-limit}
\end{equation}
Writing $V\phi_j=\omega_j\phi_j$ makes the shift explicit:
\begin{align}
    \|G+K\Delta\|^2
    ={}&\sum_{j\geq1}\omega_jZ_j^2
       +2\sum_{j\geq1}\sqrt{\omega_j}Z_j
          \langle\phi_j,K\Delta\rangle
       +\|K\Delta\|^2.
    \label{eq:inference-local-expansion}
\end{align}
Hence detectability is governed by the covariance-weighted displacement
$K\Delta$, not by $\|\Delta\|$ alone.  Under homoskedasticity, if
$\Delta=\sum_j d_jv_j$ in the eigenbasis of $K$, then
\begin{equation}
    \|K\Delta\|^2
    =\sum_{j\geq1}\lambda_j^2d_j^2.
    \label{eq:inference-detectability}
\end{equation}
For alternatives of comparable coefficient norm, the deterministic shift is
less attenuated in leading covariance directions and such departures are
typically easier to detect than departures concentrated in weak directions.
More generally, local power depends on the orientation of $K\Delta$ relative
to the covariance operator $V$, not on $\|K\Delta\|$ alone.  This is a
pointwise local-power statement; it does not imply uniform power over all
unit-norm directions in an ill-posed problem.  A proof of the fixed and local
claims is given in Proposition~\ref{prop:app-inference-alternatives}.

The finite-sample power experiment uses the perturbation family
\begin{equation}
    \beta_\delta(s)=\beta(s)+\delta\Delta(s),
    \qquad
    \Delta(s)=s,
    \qquad
    \delta\in\{-1,-0.95,\ldots,0.95,1\}.
    \label{eq:inference-power-family}
\end{equation}
The tested candidate remains the baseline $b=\beta$.  Thus $\delta=0$ is the
null and every fixed non-zero $\delta$ is a fixed alternative at a given
sample size.  It corresponds to the theoretical local sequence only if
$\delta= c/\sqrt n$ for a fixed constant $c$.  This distinction prevents the
simulation grid from being mistaken for the literal triangular alternative
in \eqref{eq:inference-local-alternative}.

To make the large experiment computationally feasible, power is calculated
with the direct statistic $\mathcal S_n(b)$ in
\eqref{eq:inference-direct-statistic}, evaluated exactly in the generating
score coordinates.  It is not obtained by running 70 CG iterations for every
alternative replication.  The reported curves therefore describe the
finite-sample power of the direct-moment target, which is the first-order limit
of sufficiently late-stopped inference; they are not finite-$m=70$ power
curves.  The stopping experiment separately assesses the approximation under
the null designs.  At the 5\% level, empirical null quantiles are computed for
each model and for $n\in\{100,200\}$.  Monte Carlo standard errors for the
resulting rejection frequencies use the binomial formula in
\eqref{eq:simulation-mcse}.  These standard errors condition on the realised
simulated critical values; they do not propagate the additional Monte Carlo
uncertainty from estimating an oracle or empirical-null quantile.

\section{Confidence sets by test inversion}
\label{sec:inference-confidence}

Any level-$\alpha$ test can be inverted.  On an identified parameter space
$\Theta\subseteq\mathcal H_{\mathrm{id}}$, define
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal C_{1-\alpha}
    =\left\{b\in\Theta:
      \mathcal T_n(b)\leq q_{1-\alpha}\right\}.
    \label{eq:inference-confidence-set}
\end{equation}
If the test has asymptotic size $\alpha$, then
$\mathbb P_\beta\{\beta\in\mathcal C_{1-\alpha}\}\to1-\alpha$; see
Corollary~\ref{cor:app-confidence-inversion}.  Without the identification
restriction, the set is invariant along $\operatorname{Null}(K)$ and describes
an equivalence class rather than a unique slope.  In a finite sample,
$\widehat K$ is itself finite rank, so unrestricted directions in
$\operatorname{Null}(\widehat K)$ also make the raw inverted set unbounded.

For visualisation, the computation restricts candidate functions to a
$p$-dimensional space
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal H_p
    =\operatorname{span}\{h_1,\ldots,h_p\},
    \qquad
    b_c(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}c_jh_j(s),
    \label{eq:inference-truncated-space}
\end{equation}
using the first $p=5$ cosine functions from the simulation basis.  Let $H$ be
the $T\times p$ matrix whose $j$th column contains $h_j$ on the numerical
grid, let
\begin{equation}
    A=\widehat K H,
    \qquad
    f=\widehat K\widehat\beta_{m_C}^{\mathrm{CG}},
    \label{eq:inference-confidence-matrices}
\end{equation}
and recall that the discrete squared $L^2$ norm is $T^{-1}\|\cdot\|_2^2$.
The accepted coefficient set is exactly
\begin{equation}
    C_{1-\alpha}^{(p)}
    =\left\{c\in\mathbb R^p:
       \frac nT\|f-Ac\|_2^2\leq q_{1-\alpha}
      \right\}.
    \label{eq:inference-coefficient-set}
\end{equation}

When $A$ has full column rank, let
\begin{equation}
    \widehat c=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top f,
    \qquad
    \rho^2=\frac{Tq_{1-\alpha}}n-
             \|f-A\widehat c\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:inference-ellipsoid-parameters}
\end{equation}
If $\rho^2<0$, the restricted set is empty.  Otherwise it is the ellipsoid
\begin{equation}
    (c-\widehat c)^\top A^\top A(c-\widehat c)
    \leq\rho^2.
    \label{eq:inference-confidence-ellipsoid}
\end{equation}
For the row vector $h(s)^\top=(h_1(s),\ldots,h_p(s))$, its pointwise extrema
are
\begin{equation}
    h(s)^\top\widehat c
    \mathbin{\pm}
    \left\{
      \rho^2 h(s)^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}h(s)
    \right\}^{1/2}.
    \label{eq:inference-envelope}
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:app-confidence-ellipsoid} derives
\eqref{eq:inference-confidence-ellipsoid}--\eqref{eq:inference-envelope} and
records the rank-deficient cases.  This analytic calculation gives the exact
envelope of the full ellipsoid in $\mathbb R^5$, without the notebook's
additional coefficient-box restriction.  It replaces the reference
notebook's discrete search over $20^5$ coefficient vectors in $[0,4.5]^5$;
the two algorithms therefore illustrate the same test-inversion idea but do
not define identical finite grids.

The confidence experiment uses $m_C=10$, retaining the reference notebook's
separate choice for this illustration, whereas the null-law experiment uses
$m_{\mathrm{late}}=70$.  Choosing ten components does not by itself prove
\eqref{eq:inference-late-stopping-condition}; its adequacy must be judged from
finite-sample coverage and residual diagnostics.  Thus $m_C=10$ reproduces a
feature of the reference illustration; it is not presented as a generally
valid inference-stopping recommendation.  Its formal confidence interpretation
therefore remains conditional on the moment residual being sufficiently small.
Nor is the five-dimensional display the full functional confidence set: the
data-generating slopes have
non-zero coefficients beyond the fifth basis function.  Three reported
summaries are therefore related but different:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item non-rejection when the test statistic is evaluated at the full true
          simulated slope;
    \item acceptance of the true slope's five-term projection within
          $\mathcal H_5$; and
    \item the lower and upper pointwise envelope of the accepted
          five-dimensional coefficient set.
\end{enumerate}
The shaded region drawn in Chapter~\ref{chap:inference-results} is formed from
the pointwise medians of 5,000 lower and upper envelopes.  It is a Monte Carlo
summary of many realised sets, not one observed-sample 95\% confidence band.
Visual containment of the true curve in that median region is consequently not
a coverage statement.  The full-slope acceptance indicator deliberately reuses
the null-size data stream, but it is evaluated at $m_C=10$ rather than
$m_{\mathrm{late}}=70$.  Its average is therefore closely comparable with,
but not algebraically complementary to, the reported late-stopped size.
Acceptance and rejection are exact complements only when the same stopping
index and critical value are used; the reused samples do not provide an
independent validation of the size result.

\label{sec:inference-implementation}

Sections~\ref{sec:simulation-dgp} and \ref{sec:simulation-models}.  It therefore
inherits the same 100-term Gaussian score expansion, 200-point grid, Babii
cosine convention, and homoskedastic error standard deviation one.  Reusing
the design connects the inferential evidence to the estimation mechanisms
already isolated, while using a separate seed prevents accidental dependence
between the two Monte Carlo archives.

listed replication count is per model and, for power, per sample size and

For the stopping branch, the adaptive comparator uses the same
estimation-oriented discrepancy formula and constants as
Chapter~\ref{chap:methods}, with $\tau=1.01$ and
$\delta_{\mathrm{stop}}=0.1$.  Its internal FPCR moment-GCV variance pilot
uses \eqref{eq:simulation-moment-gcv}--\eqref{eq:simulation-cg-sigma-pilot},
but is capped at 20 components, compared with 70 in the estimation experiment.
It also uses the stabilised moment-residual path described below in place of
the estimation study's short recurrence.  The stopping experiment therefore
assesses an estimation-oriented variant; it does not reproduce the complete
Chapter~\ref{chap:estimation-results} procedure unchanged.  The pilot cap can
alter the variance estimate, discrepancy threshold, and selected index.
Writing
$\delta_{\mathrm{stop}}$ distinguishes this tuning constant from the power
perturbation $\delta$ in \eqref{eq:inference-power-family}.  The maximum-rank
benchmark projects $\widehat r$ onto the numerical range of $\widehat K$.
For every stopping rule, the diagnostics retain
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt n\|e_m\|,
    \qquad
    |\mathcal T_{n,m}(b)-\mathcal S_n(b)|,
    \qquad
    \mathbf 1\{\mathcal T_{n,m}(b)>q_{0.95}\},
    \label{eq:inference-stopping-diagnostics}
\end{equation}
as well as the selected or fixed index.  Together these quantities show
whether a path has approached the inferential target and what size distortion
remains; merely reporting $m$ would not.

The reported archive uses a numerically stabilised realisation of the
moment-residual path.  At each dimension, a two-pass reorthogonalisation forms
an orthonormal basis $Q_m$ for
$\widehat{\mathcal K}_m(\widehat K,\widehat r)$, after which a QR solve computes
\begin{equation}
    \widehat a_m
    \in\arg\min_{a\in\mathbb R^m}
       \|\widehat r-\widehat KQ_ma\|_2,
    \qquad
    \widehat\beta_m^{\mathrm{CG}}=Q_m\widehat a_m.
    \label{eq:inference-stable-engine}
\end{equation}
In exact arithmetic this solves the same nested minimum-moment-residual
problem as the released recurrence; the coordinate system and finite-precision
path are stabilised because inference deliberately proceeds much farther than
the estimation rule.  This solver is distinct from the Arnoldi FPLS estimator
in Chapter~\ref{chap:methods}: both use an orthogonal Krylov basis,
but Arnoldi FPLS minimises response error, whereas
\eqref{eq:inference-stable-engine} retains the CG--FPLS moment-residual
objective.  Propositions~\ref{prop:app-arnoldi-span} and
\ref{prop:app-response-moment-krylov} establish the relevant span and objective
distinctions.

All random quantities derive reproducibly from the master seed 2025.  Dedicated
streams separate the stopping experiment and the alternative power samples,
while several reuses are intentional: the confidence experiment regenerates
the null-size samples, the $n=100$ empirical power calibration reuses the
direct null statistics, and the oracle draws serve all relevant branches.  The
archive retains the configuration, replication-level arrays, summary tables,
and vector figures.  The executable command and output manifest are recorded
in the reproducibility notes rather than embedded in the methodological
argument.  This separation is deliberate: the chapter fixes the estimand,
statistic, approximation, and calibration, while the archive makes their
numerical implementation auditable.

This ordering is essential for interpreting the evidence that follows.
Before rejection frequencies can be attributed to calibration or power, the
iterative approximation must be shown to be negligible.  Chapter
\ref{chap:inference-results} therefore begins with the stopping diagnostics and
then turns to null size, power, and confidence-set behaviour.
